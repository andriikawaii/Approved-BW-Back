H2: Split-Level and Colonial Kitchens in Orange: What to Expect
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Orange's residential character is defined by split-level and colonial homes built from the 1950s through the 1980s, and each housing type presents distinct kitchen renovation conditions that a contractor working in town needs to understand before the consultation ends.

In split-level homes, the kitchen sits on the entry level, positioned between the lower family room and the upper bedroom floor. The footprint was designed for 1960s household function: often a galley or partial-open layout, compact by today's standards, with minimal sightlines to the living areas. Opening that plan to the dining room or family room almost always involves a load-bearing wall. That means a structural assessment, stamped architectural or engineering plans, and a building permit before demolition begins. We assess what is structural at the consultation, not after the demo crew has already started work.

Colonial homes from the 1960s and 1980s typically offer a more conventional platform-framed structure and larger kitchen footprints than split-levels. Layout changes in colonials are often more straightforward to engineer, but older electrical panels and outdated plumbing are just as common here as in split-levels.

Both housing types share one near-universal condition in Orange: electrical panels that are undersized for modern kitchen loads. A kitchen with an induction range, refrigerator, dishwasher, microwave, and under-cabinet lighting will draw more current than most 100-amp panels were designed to support. We include panel assessment in every Orange kitchen consultation and build the upgrade into the scope when it is needed.
