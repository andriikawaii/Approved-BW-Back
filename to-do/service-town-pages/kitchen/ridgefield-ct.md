H2: What Is Included in a Kitchen Remodel
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
A complete kitchen remodel covers every trade from demolition through final paint, including all required permits, inspections, and daily cleanup throughout the project.

We begin with demolition and removal of existing cabinetry, countertops, and flooring. Before any new material goes in, we assess what is behind the walls. Connecticut homes built before 2000 regularly turn up outdated electrical panels, deteriorated plumbing, or water damage inside wall cavities. In Ridgefield's 1950s through 1970s homes, 100-amp electrical panels are common. When walls are opened for kitchen work, upgrading to 200-amp service is code-required. We assess panel capacity during the planning phase and include the upgrade in scope if needed.

New cabinetry installation includes soft-close hardware and adjustable shelving as standard. Countertop fabrication and installation, backsplash tile, and flooring with proper underlayment are part of the scope. Electrical work covers new circuits for appliances, under-cabinet lighting, and updated outlet and switch locations. Plumbing covers the sink, dishwasher, and garbage disposal connections, along with any relocated water lines. We coordinate appliance delivery and installation directly.

Interior painting within the kitchen, drywall patching, and daily cleanup are included throughout. We install dust barriers to protect the rest of your home during construction. The project closes with a final walkthrough where we go through every detail together before calling the work complete.

H2: What Makes Ridgefield Kitchen Remodeling Different
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Ridgefield's rural character, larger lots, and granite bedrock create project conditions that matter when scoping a kitchen remodel accurately.

The town's residential zones are predominantly two-acre and larger. Homes here have more square footage than in many Fairfield County towns, and kitchen footprints reflect that. Ridgefield kitchens are often the center of larger open floor plan configurations, with defined cooking, dining, and family room zones that homeowners choose to rework. These are not the compact galley kitchens common in coastal beach houses. Opening up or reconfiguring these spaces frequently involves structural work, and we scope that correctly from the start rather than discovering it mid-project.

Rock ledge is a defining characteristic of Ridgefield's landscape. The town sits on granite bedrock, and ledge conditions are common throughout, particularly on elevated lots. For a kitchen project that stays within the existing footprint (cabinetry, countertops, flooring, plumbing and electrical updates), ledge does not affect the scope. For any kitchen addition or excavation work adjacent to the house, a ledge contingency belongs in the project plan. We assess site conditions and include this discussion during planning, before any scope is finalized. This keeps the budget conversation honest from the beginning.

The historic town center on Ridgefield's Main Street falls within a Historic District. Homes within the district require a Certificate of Appropriateness from the Historic District Commission for exterior changes before a building permit can proceed. Interior kitchen work does not typically trigger HDC review, but any project that involves exterior modifications (a window addition, a bump-out, or exterior materials changes visible from the street) requires that approval step first. We identify whether your property falls within the district during our initial consultation and build the HDC timeline into the project schedule if it applies.

Ridgefield Building Department issues separate building, electrical, and plumbing permits. Structural changes require plans stamped by a Connecticut-licensed architect or engineer. We handle all permit applications and inspection coordination as part of every project.
