H2: Kitchen Remodeling Costs in Greenwich, CT
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Kitchen remodeling in Greenwich starts at $40,000 for a minor refresh and reaches $400,000 or more for a full custom renovation with structural changes and premium materials. Those costs reflect the town's housing stock complexity, permit requirements, and material expectations.

The table below reflects what projects in Greenwich actually cost in 2025. These ranges account for the starting point of material quality in this market, which begins at a tier most other Connecticut towns would consider an upgrade.

| Tier | Scope | Typical Range |
|------|-------|---------------|
| Minor Refresh | Cabinet doors, countertops, appliances, paint | $40,000 – $80,000 |
| Major Mid-Range | New cabinets, floors, all appliances, layout update | $90,000 – $140,000 |
| Full Custom | Full custom, structural changes, premium materials | $200,000 – $400,000+ |

Several factors drive Greenwich kitchen costs above statewide averages. Material expectations here start at Sub-Zero, Wolf, and Waterworks, not builder-grade product. Custom millwork to match original ovolo, ogee, and beaded molding profiles in pre-war homes adds both material cost and lead time. And any project involving below-grade work or an addition should carry a ledge rock contingency of $20,000 to $80,000 depending on what the excavation reveals.

Custom cabinet orders run 8 to 12 weeks from order placement to delivery. Semi-custom cabinets reduce that window to 4 to 6 weeks. We finalize all selections and place orders early in the planning phase so cabinet lead time runs concurrently with permit review rather than delaying your construction start.

H2: What Is Included in a Greenwich Kitchen Remodel
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
A complete kitchen remodel in Greenwich covers every trade from demolition through final paint: cabinetry, countertops, backsplash tile, flooring, electrical, plumbing, appliance installation, structural modifications where required, and all permits and inspections.

We begin with a thorough assessment before any demo happens. Greenwich kitchens in pre-war homes consistently reveal conditions that need to be addressed before finish work can begin: balloon framing without fire blocking at the floor line, plaster-on-lath walls that require different demo and repair approaches than drywall, undersized electrical panels that cannot carry modern appliance loads, and plumbing supply and drain lines that have not been touched since original installation. We document everything we find and bring it into the scope before it becomes a mid-project surprise.

Structural modifications are part of the work in many Greenwich kitchens. Load-bearing wall removal to open a floor plan, beam installation, and header work are handled under our HIC license with structural engineering coordination where the scope requires it. For pre-1940 homes, fire blocking installation in opened balloon-framed walls is included as a code requirement, not an add-on.

Cabinetry installation includes soft-close hardware and adjustable shelving throughout. In homes with original millwork adjacent to the kitchen, we assess the existing profiles and arrange custom milling where standard profiles from a lumber yard will not match. Countertop fabrication and installation, backsplash tile work, and all electrical (new circuits for refrigerators, ranges, dishwashers, and microwaves, plus under-cabinet and ambient lighting) are part of every full-scope project. Plumbing includes sink and dishwasher connections, disposal installation, and any relocated supply or drain lines. Appliance coordination and installation are included.

Interior painting within the kitchen footprint, drywall repair and skim coating, dust barriers to protect the rest of your home during construction, and daily job site cleanup are standard throughout every project. The project concludes with a final walkthrough where we go through every detail together before calling it complete.

H2: Greenwich Permitting: What the Process Actually Requires
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Kitchen remodeling in Greenwich requires building, electrical, and plumbing permits, with fees assessed separately and multi-department sign-offs required before the building permit is issued. The process can take weeks for simple interiors and several months for projects involving structural changes.

The building permit fee is $13.26 per $1,000 of renovation budget. Electrical and plumbing permits each carry a separate fee of $12 per $1,000. If your home was built before 1940, a 5% surcharge applies to the permit fee. On a $150,000 kitchen renovation in a pre-war Old Greenwich colonial, these fees represent a meaningful budget line that needs to be accounted for from the start, not discovered at permit application.

Before a building permit is issued, four departments review independently: Zoning, Health, the Fire Marshal, and the Department of Public Works. Each has its own review cycle. On a straightforward interior kitchen renovation the multi-department process may clear in a few weeks. On a project involving structural changes, layout modifications that affect plumbing or egress, or any exterior work, the review can take several months. Your project schedule needs to be built around realistic approval timelines, not optimistic ones.

If your home falls within Greenwich's Historic Overlay (HO) or Historic Residential Overlay (HRO) district, a Certificate of Appropriateness from the Historic District Commission is required for any exterior change before the building permit application can proceed. This applies to windows, doors, siding, and in some cases paint selection. Greenwich has six historic districts town-wide. For interior-only kitchen projects in a historically designated home, the HDC review may not be required, but we confirm that during the planning phase.

Inspection scheduling in Greenwich uses the City Squared portal, which has been the town's platform since February 2025. Framing, electrical rough-in, plumbing rough-in, and final inspections are all scheduled through this system. We handle all permit applications, department coordination, HDC submissions where applicable, and every inspection request. You should not have to navigate this process on your own.

H2: The Structural Profile of Pre-War Greenwich Kitchens
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Pre-war Greenwich homes, the colonials, Tudor revivals, French Provincials, and shingle-style houses that define the town's architectural character, carry structural conditions that contractors without genuine experience in this housing stock regularly underprice and underscope.

Balloon framing is the dominant structural system in Greenwich homes built before the 1940s. In balloon-framed construction, studs run continuously from the foundation sill to the roof ridge without fire blocking at the floor lines. When any wall in these homes is opened for any reason (running new electrical, relocating plumbing, adding insulation), fire blocking installation at each floor level is required before the wall is closed. This is not optional or a judgment call; it is a code requirement. Contractors who are unfamiliar with balloon framing either miss it entirely or treat it as an unexpected cost after demo begins. We include it in the scope.

Plaster-on-lath construction is standard in pre-1940 Greenwich interiors. Plaster behaves differently than drywall in demolition, generates significantly more debris, and requires a different repair approach where selective removal is planned. In a full kitchen gut, we remove it completely. In a partial renovation, we assess which sections are sound and which are compromised and make targeted decisions. Plaster patch repairs that are not done correctly fail visibly and quickly. We do not leave that behind.

Original millwork profiles in pre-war Greenwich kitchens present a specific challenge when a kitchen project is adjacent to existing formal rooms or hallways with original trim. Ovolo, ogee, and beaded profiles from the 1890s through the 1930s are not available from any Connecticut lumber yard. When matching is required, we arrange custom milling. It adds cost and lead time, and the result is a transition that reads correctly rather than one that announces itself as wrong to anyone who looks closely.

Rock ledge throughout elevated areas north and west of the Post Road affects any Greenwich project that involves below-grade work, additions, or excavation. The fractured granite that characterizes this geology cannot be blasted; it requires hydraulic hammering, which is slower and more expensive. If your kitchen project includes an addition, a relocated exterior door with new footings, or any other excavation component, a ledge contingency of $20,000 to $80,000 should be in the budget. We discuss this before we write the proposal, not after excavation begins.

Stone foundations in pre-1920 Greenwich homes, particularly in Cos Cob along the Mianus River and in coastal Old Greenwich and Riverside, are subject to water infiltration and hydrostatic pressure. For kitchen projects that involve any work near the lower level or foundation, moisture assessment comes before framing.

── Step 3: Selections ────────────────────────────────────

We guide you through material choices: cabinets, countertops, tile, and flooring, with options at different price points.

In Greenwich, selections often begin at a tier that includes semi-custom or full custom cabinetry, stone countertops, and premium hardware. In pre-war Greenwich homes, demo frequently reveals conditions inside walls that were not visible during the assessment. We walk through everything together. If anything needs attention, we address it before calling the project complete.
