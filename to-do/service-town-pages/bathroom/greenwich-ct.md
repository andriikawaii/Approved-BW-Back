Galvanized supply line replacement, which we recommend whenever walls are open and galvanized lines are present, is work that belongs in the scope from day one rather than discovered mid-project as a change order.

H2: What Is Included in a Greenwich Bathroom Remodel
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
A complete bathroom remodel in Greenwich covers every element from demolition through final inspection: waterproofing, tile, shower or tub, vanity, plumbing, electrical, ventilation, and all required permits, managed as a single coordinated project.

We begin with full demolition of existing tile, fixtures, vanity, and flooring. In pre-war Greenwich homes, that demo reveals conditions that need to be addressed before any finish work goes in. Plaster walls require a different demolition approach than drywall, they generate significantly more debris volume and need proper substrate preparation before any tile adhesive is applied. Subfloor condition is assessed; tile systems require solid, deflection-free support, and floors in homes of this age often need reinforcement before tile installation begins.

Waterproofing is installed using membrane systems behind all shower and tub tile. Cement board is standard in all wet areas. In Greenwich's older homes, where the presence of moisture-damaged framing behind original tile is common, we document what we find behind the walls before waterproofing begins. If there is rot or structural compromise, we address it at that point rather than covering it with new material.

Plumbing work includes new supply and drain lines where galvanized pipe is found. When walls are open, we strongly recommend full replacement of galvanized supply lines regardless of visible condition, because corrosion inside galvanized pipe reduces water pressure and produces discolored water that only gets worse over time. New connections for fixtures, the shower or tub, and any relocated plumbing are included in the scope. Electrical work covers new lighting circuits, GFCI outlets to current code, exhaust fan installation ducted to the exterior, and where required, panel coordination for bathroom circuits that need dedicated capacity. Every project includes permit coordination, daily cleanup, dust barriers to protect adjacent rooms, and a final walkthrough together.

H2: What Pre-War Greenwich Homes Require in a Bathroom Remodel
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Pre-1940 Greenwich homes represent over 35% of the town's housing stock, and those homes carry bathroom conditions that contractors without genuine experience in this building era regularly miss or underprice.

Cast iron tubs in pre-war Greenwich bathrooms are original to the home in many cases. These tubs weigh 300 to 400 pounds and often cannot be removed in one piece without damaging surrounding framing or flooring. The standard approach is to section the tub inside the bathroom using an angle grinder and remove it in pieces. This is planned labor, not a surprise, but it needs to be in the scope from the beginning. Contractors who do not work regularly in pre-war homes sometimes include this as a change order after demo begins.

Galvanized supply lines are the other condition most consistently found in Greenwich bathrooms of this era. Galvanized pipe corrodes from the inside out over decades. The visible symptoms are low water pressure and discolored water, but the pipe may be near failure even without visible exterior rust. When bathroom walls are open for any reason, we strongly recommend full replacement of galvanized lines with copper or PEX. The cost of doing this work while walls are already open is a fraction of what it costs to open them again later. We include this recommendation in every pre-war bathroom proposal and defer only to your decision.

Electrical conditions in the oldest Greenwich homes require particular attention. Homes with original knob-and-tube wiring present GFCI upgrade requirements in bathroom circuits, and in some cases that work surfaces panel deficiencies that require coordination with an electrician on panel capacity. Bathroom electrical work in all Connecticut homes requires an electrical permit. In pre-war Greenwich homes, this step should be scoped carefully before construction begins rather than assessed mid-project. Plaster walls, which are standard in pre-1940 Greenwich interiors, generate significantly more demo debris than drywall. For full gut renovations, complete removal down to studs is the correct approach. For selective demo, we assess which sections are sound and make targeted decisions rather than removing more than necessary.

H2: Greenwich Permitting for Bathroom Remodeling
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
Bathroom remodeling in Greenwich requires separate building, electrical, and plumbing permits, with fees assessed independently for each and inspection scheduling through the City Squared portal.

The building permit fee is $13.26 per $1,000 of renovation budget. Electrical and plumbing permits each carry a separate fee of $12 per $1,000. For homes built before 1940, a 5% surcharge applies to the total permit fee. On a $60,000 bathroom renovation in a pre-war Riverside colonial, those fees represent a real budget item that should be calculated at the outset rather than discovered at permit application.

Historic District Commission review is not typically required for bathroom interior renovations, even in homes within one of Greenwich's six historic districts. HDC review applies to exterior changes. If your project involves any exterior component: a window change for a shower conversion, for example, we confirm HDC applicability during the planning phase. Inspection scheduling for all trades uses the City Squared portal, in place since February 2025. We handle all permit applications, department coordination, and every inspection request as a standard part of every project. You should not have to navigate that process independently.

H2: Frequently Asked Questions: Bathroom Remodeling in Greenwich
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Q: Do I need a permit for a bathroom remodel in Greenwich?

A: Yes. Bathroom remodeling in Greenwich requires separate building, electrical, and plumbing permits, each assessed and inspected independently. The building permit fee is $13.26 per $1,000 of renovation budget. Electrical and plumbing permits carry a separate fee of $12 per $1,000 each. A 5% surcharge applies to all permit fees for homes built before 1940. Inspection scheduling uses the City Squared portal, which has been Greenwich's platform since February 2025. Historic District Commission review is not typically required for bathroom interior work, even in historically designated homes. We handle all permit applications, multi-department coordination, and every inspection as a standard part of every project.

──

Q: How long does a bathroom remodel take in Greenwich?

A: Most full bathroom renovations in Greenwich complete in three to six weeks of active construction after permits are issued and materials are on site. Basic refreshes run two to three weeks. Full gut renovations with custom tile, a new shower enclosure, and a new vanity typically run four to six weeks. Projects that involve layout changes, plumbing relocation, or room expansion extend beyond that. In Greenwich, the permit process involves separate filings for building, electrical, and plumbing through City Squared, and lead times on custom tile, steam units, and specialty fixtures are common. We include all of those timelines in your written project schedule so you know your realistic start and finish dates before construction begins.

──

Q: Can I use the bathroom during the remodel?

A: Not the bathroom being renovated. We seal off the work area with dust barriers, and the space will not be functional until the project is complete. If you have a second bathroom in your home, you will be able to use that throughout the project. If the bathroom being renovated is your only bathroom, we discuss that directly during the planning phase and work through the options before construction starts, whether that means sequencing work to restore basic function as quickly as possible or making other temporary arrangements. We make sure you understand access conditions before a single day of demo begins.

──

Q: What is the difference between a bathroom refresh and a full gut renovation?

A: A refresh updates fixtures and surfaces without opening walls or changing the layout, typically a new vanity, faucets, flooring, and paint, with plumbing and drain lines remaining in place. It generally starts around $18,000 in Greenwich. A full gut renovation takes the space down to the studs. That allows for proper waterproofing behind all tile, subfloor repairs, plumbing relocation, galvanized line replacement, and a complete redesign of the space. In Greenwich's pre-war homes, a full gut also allows for the cast iron tub removal, plaster substrate correction, and electrical updating that a surface refresh cannot address. Full gut renovations in Greenwich start around $35,000 and go up from there based on scope and selections. The right choice depends on what is behind your current walls as much as what you want in front of them.

──

Q: Can you install a walk-in shower, radiant heat floors, or a freestanding tub in a Greenwich bathroom?

A: Yes. These are among the most common requests we receive for Greenwich bathroom projects, and we handle every trade involved. Walk-in shower installation includes plumbing reconfiguration, drain placement with correct slope for barrier-free entry, waterproofing membrane, tile, and the shower enclosure. Radiant heat floor installation requires the appropriate subfloor depth for the heating element and flooring system selected, and it is best coordinated when the floor is already opened during a full gut renovation. Freestanding tub installation involves both supply and drain work, as these fixtures typically require floor-mounted connections rather than wall-mounted. A building permit is required for walk-in shower conversions in Greenwich, and plumbing permits cover supply and drain work. We manage all permit applications and inspections as part of the project.
